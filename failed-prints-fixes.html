Failure-Based Learning

Failed Prints & Fixes

Use failed builds as engineering evidence. A failed print, cut, or toolpath is not the end of the process—it is data for your next iteration.

Common Fabrication Problems

Poor Bed Adhesion

What you may see: The print lifts, curls, or slides during the first layers.

Try next: Clean the build plate, check orientation, use a larger flat surface, and confirm correct filament settings.

Too Many Supports

What you may see: Rough surfaces, wasted material, or difficult removal.

Try next: Reorient the model, split the part, or redesign unsupported overhangs.

Laser Burn Marks

What you may see: Dark edges, scorched surface, or excessive smoke marks.

Try next: Mask the material, check speed/power settings, and use approved materials only.

CNC Workholding Failure

What you may see: Material shifts, chatter, broken tools, or inaccurate cuts.

Try next: Stop immediately, improve clamps/fixtures, verify zero, and ask before restarting.

Weak Solder Joint

What you may see: Dull, cracked, loose, or unreliable electrical connections.

Try next: Reheat correctly, use clean contact points, avoid excess solder, and inspect before powering.

Wrong File Type

What you may see: The tool software cannot open the file or the geometry imports incorrectly.

Try next: Check the tool page for required file types before submitting.

How to Document a Failure

  • Describe what happened.
  • Identify the likely cause.
  • Record the settings, material, or design feature involved.
  • Explain what you will change next.
  • Test the improvement and compare the result.

Engineering Mindset

A failed attempt is useful when you learn from it. Your goal is not to hide failure—it is to use it to make a stronger design.
